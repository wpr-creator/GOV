1.03 · FOUNDATIONS OF AMERICAN DEMOCRACY

THE HISTORY LESSON

ESSENTIAL QUESTION How did the United States replace a weak national government without creating another tyranny?

Follow the story from independence to the Bill of Rights. At every stop, notice the problem, the response, and what happened next.

THE CENTRAL CHALLENGE

POWERFUL ENOUGH TO GOVERN.
LIMITED ENOUGH TO PROTECT LIBERTY.

TOO LITTLE POWERGovernment cannot collect money, enforce laws, or protect order.
TOO MUCH POWERGovernment can abuse its authority and violate people’s rights.

Americans had to build a government between these two dangers.

SECTION 011776

INDEPENDENCE

THE BREAK FROM BRITAIN

The Declaration of Independence announced that the colonies were leaving Britain. It also accused King George III of repeatedly abusing power and denying the colonists a voice in government.

THE FEARAmericans did not want another tyranny—a government that uses power unfairly and ignores people’s rights.

WHAT CAME NEXT? The new states needed a government of their own.

SECTION 021781

THE FIRST GOVERNMENT

THE ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION

The Articles created a confederation—a partnership in which the states kept most political power. Congress represented the country, but it depended on the states for money and cooperation. Americans designed it this way because they feared replacing a powerful king with a powerful national government.

CONGRESS COULD

  • Declare war
  • Make treaties
  • Coin money
  • Handle foreign relations

THE SYSTEM HAD

  • No president to enforce laws
  • No national courts to settle disputes
  • One vote for each state, regardless of population
  • No direct power to collect taxes from citizens

WHAT CAME NEXT? A government designed to be weak had trouble doing its job.

SECTION 031780s

THE PROBLEMS

A GOVERNMENT TOO WEAK TO ACT

  1. NO TAX POWERCongress could ask states for money, but it could not require payment. Without dependable revenue—money coming in—it struggled to pay debts and support an army.
  2. NO TRADE POWERStates placed taxes and restrictions on one another’s goods. Congress could not create one national rule for trade between states.
  3. NO ENFORCEMENTCongress could make decisions, but there was no president or executive branch responsible for carrying them out.
  4. NO NATIONAL COURTSThere was no federal court system to settle disagreements between states or explain what national laws meant.
  5. NO STABLE MONEYStates printed different money. This made prices, debts, and trade difficult to manage across the country.

SHAYS’ REBELLION

Massachusetts farmers faced heavy taxes and debt. Courts could take their farms or jail them when they could not pay. Daniel Shays and other armed protesters closed courthouses to stop those cases. The national government could not respond quickly or raise dependable money for troops.

THE LESSONA government can be too weak to protect liberty, property, and public order.

SECTION 041787

A NEW PLAN

THE CONSTITUTIONAL CONVENTION

Delegates from twelve states met in Philadelphia planning to revise, or repair, the Articles. After debating the country’s problems, they decided that small changes would not be enough. They proposed an entirely new Constitution with more national power and new limits on how that power could be used.

CONGRESS COULD NOT TAXCongress received the power to collect taxes.

CONGRESS COULD NOT CONTROL TRADECongress received power over trade between states and with other countries.

NO EXECUTIVE BRANCHArticle II created a president to enforce federal law.

NO NATIONAL COURTSArticle III created a federal court system.

STATES COULD IGNORE DECISIONSThe Supremacy Clause made valid federal law the highest law of the land.

LIMITS ON THE NEW POWER

The Constitution separated power among three branches, gave each branch ways to check the others, and divided authority between the national and state governments. The goal was not simply to create a stronger government. It was to create a government strong enough to function while making it difficult for one person or group to control everything.

SECTION 051787

THE DEALS

MAJOR COMPROMISES

GREAT COMPROMISE

Representation would be based on population in the House. Every state would have two senators.

THREE-FIFTHS COMPROMISE

For representation and direct taxes, every five enslaved people were counted as three people. Enslaved people had no political rights, but this increased the power of slave states.

SLAVE-TRADE COMPROMISE

Congress could not ban the international slave trade before 1808. The agreement protected the trade for twenty more years.

ELECTORAL COLLEGE

States would appoint electors who formally choose the president instead of using one national popular vote.

ARTICLE V

The Constitution could be amended without every state agreeing, but change would still be difficult.

IMPORTANT: The Constitution came from political ideals and political deals. Some compromises protected slavery and caused lasting harm.

SECTION 061787–88

THE RATIFICATION DEBATE

WOULD THE NEW PLAN PROTECT LIBERTY?

Before the Constitution could take effect, nine states had to ratify, or formally approve, it. Americans argued about which danger was greater: a weak government or a powerful one.

FEDERALISTS SUPPORTED IT

Federalists believed the Articles were too weak. The country needed a national government that could collect taxes, regulate trade, enforce laws, and protect order.

In Federalist No. 10, James Madison argued that a large republic could make it harder for one faction—an interest group—to take control. Federalists also said separated powers and checks and balances would limit government.

ANTI-FEDERALISTS OPPOSED IT

Anti-Federalists feared the new national government would become too powerful and too distant from ordinary people. They worried that states would lose authority and that individual rights were not clearly protected.

In Brutus No. 1, an Anti-Federalist writer warned that a large republic might not represent the people well and that national power could continue to grow.

THE SHARED GOALBoth sides wanted to protect liberty. They disagreed about which kind of government would protect it best.

SECTION 071788–91

THE FINAL AGREEMENT

RATIFICATION AND THE BILL OF RIGHTS

1788The ninth state ratified the Constitution.

1789The new government began operating.

1791The states ratified the first ten amendments: the Bill of Rights.

Federalists had promised to add written protections for individual liberty. The Bill of Rights helped answer one of the Anti-Federalists’ strongest objections.

ANSWER TO THE ESSENTIAL QUESTIONThe Constitution gave the national government enough power to act. It also divided and limited that power. The Bill of Rights added specific protections for individual liberty.
