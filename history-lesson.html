1.03 · FOUNDATIONS OF AMERICAN DEMOCRACY

THE HISTORY LESSON

ESSENTIAL QUESTION How did the United States replace a weak national government without creating another tyranny?

Scroll through one continuous story. Each stop explains the problem Americans faced, what they tried, and what happened next.

BEGIN IN 1776 ↓
011776

INDEPENDENCE

THE BREAK FROM BRITAIN

The Declaration of Independence announced that the colonies were leaving Britain. It also accused King George III of abusing power.

Americans wanted a government strong enough to protect them, but they feared creating another ruler with too much power.

THE FEARA powerful central government might become a tyranny—government that uses power unfairly and ignores people’s rights.
021781

THE FIRST GOVERNMENT

THE ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION

The Articles created a confederation: a partnership of states with a very limited national government. The states kept most political power.

CONGRESS COULD

  • Declare war
  • Make treaties
  • Coin money
  • Handle foreign relations

THE DESIGN LIMITED POWER

  • No president
  • No national courts
  • One vote for each state
  • All 13 states had to approve an amendment

The Articles made national power weak on purpose. Soon, that weakness created new problems.

031780s

FIVE MAJOR FLAWS

A GOVERNMENT TOO WEAK TO ACT

  1. NO TAX POWERCongress could ask states for money, but it could not require payment. It struggled to pay debts or soldiers.
  2. NO TRADE POWERStates taxed and argued with one another. Congress could not set one national rule for interstate commerce—trade between states.
  3. NO ENFORCEMENTCongress could pass decisions, but it had no executive branch to carry them out.
  4. NO NATIONAL COURTSThere was no federal judicial branch to settle conflicts between states or explain national law.
  5. UNSTABLE MONEYStates printed different money, which made prices, debts, and trade confusing.

ONE MORE PROBLEM: Changing the Articles required every state to agree, so fixing the system was nearly impossible.

041786–87

THE WARNING

SHAYS’ REBELLION

Massachusetts farmers faced heavy taxes and debt. Courts could take their farms or send them to jail when they could not pay. Daniel Shays and other armed protesters shut down courts to stop those cases.

The Massachusetts government eventually stopped the rebellion, but the national government could not respond quickly or raise dependable money for troops.

THE LESSONA government can be too weak to protect liberty, property, and public order.
051787

PHILADELPHIA

A CONVENTION CHANGES COURSE

Delegates met in Philadelphia to revise, or repair, the Articles. They decided the old system could not be fixed. They began writing an entirely new Constitution.

THE BIG DISAGREEMENTS

  • Would large or small states have more influence?
  • How much power would the national government receive?
  • How directly would the people control government?
  • How would slavery affect representation and political power?
  • How could government be strong without becoming absolute?
061787

THE DEALS

MAJOR COMPROMISES

GREAT COMPROMISE

Representation by population in the House; equal representation for every state in the Senate.

THREE-FIFTHS COMPROMISE

For representation and direct taxes, every five enslaved people were counted as three people. Enslaved people had no political rights, but the agreement increased slave states’ power.

SLAVE-TRADE COMPROMISE

Congress could not ban the international slave trade before 1808. The Constitution protected the trade for twenty more years.

ELECTORAL COLLEGE

Voters do not elect the president through one national popular vote. States appoint electors who formally choose the president.

ARTICLE V

Amendments would be difficult, but they would no longer require every state to agree.

The Constitution grew from democratic ideals, practical deals, and compromises that protected slavery.

071787

A STRONGER SYSTEM

THE CONSTITUTION ANSWERS THE FLAWS

NO RELIABLE MONEY→ Congress receives the power to tax.

TRADE CONFLICTS→ Congress may regulate interstate and foreign commerce.

NO EXECUTIVE→ Article II creates the presidency to enforce federal law.

NO NATIONAL COURTS→ Article III creates a federal judiciary.

STATES IGNORE NATIONAL DECISIONS→ The Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law the highest law of the land.

UNWORKABLE AMENDMENTS→ Article V creates a demanding but possible amendment process.

081787

GUARDS AGAINST TYRANNY

ENOUGH POWER—WITH LIMITS

REPUBLICANISM
Voters choose representatives to make government decisions.
SEPARATION OF POWERS
Legislative, executive, and judicial powers belong to different branches.
CHECKS AND BALANCES
Each branch can limit certain actions of the other branches.
FEDERALISM
Power is divided between the national and state governments.
ENUMERATED POWERS
The Constitution specifically lists many powers the federal government may use.
RULE OF LAW
Government officials must obey the law too.
CIVILIAN CONTROL
Elected civilian leaders control the military.

The design assumes that people need government—and that people inside government may misuse power.

091787–88

THE RATIFICATION BATTLE

WOULD THE NEW PLAN PROTECT LIBERTY?

FEDERALISTS SAID YES

The nation needed a stronger government. A large republic could control factions—groups that pursue their own interests. Separated powers would help prevent tyranny.

READINGS: FEDERALIST 10, 51, AND 78

ANTI-FEDERALISTS SAID NO

The new government would be distant and powerful. The Necessary and Proper and Supremacy Clauses might expand national authority. The Constitution did not clearly protect individual rights.

READING: BRUTUS 1

RATIFYTo formally approve a constitution or agreement.
101788–91

THE FINAL AGREEMENT

RATIFICATION AND THE BILL OF RIGHTS

1788The ninth state ratified the Constitution. That was enough for the new system to begin.

1789The Constitution took effect. The new Congress and president began work.

1791The states ratified the first ten amendments: the Bill of Rights.

Federalists had promised amendments protecting individual liberty. The Bill of Rights originally restricted only the federal government. Most of its protections were later applied to state governments through the Fourteenth Amendment.

THE ANSWERThe Constitution gave the national government enough power to act, then divided and limited that power. The Bill of Rights added specific protections for individual liberty.
