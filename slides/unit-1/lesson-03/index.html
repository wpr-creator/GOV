THE BRIDGE

Total freedom sounded perfect—until having almost no rules created new problems. After independence, Americans also had to find a better balance.

  • The ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION protected the states from too much national control—but left the national government too weak.
  • The CONSTITUTION and RATIFICATION asked whether Americans could strengthen the government without sacrificing liberty.

[ADD SQUARE IMAGE]

Life and liberty

In 1776, the DECLARATION OF INDEPENDENCE explained why separation from Britain was justified. Government had failed because concentrated power repeatedly violated INDIVIDUAL RIGHTS.

The Declaration argued that people possessed NATURAL RIGHTS and that legitimate authority depended on consent. Through POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY, political power originated with the people.

Government remained legitimate only while it protected rights. This principle established the standard Americans later used to judge both the Articles and the Constitution.

SECTION 1 · DECLARATION OF INDEPENDENCE

Three Claims About Government

RIGHTS

People possessed NATURAL RIGHTS.

Government did not create those rights.

CONSENT

POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY placed political authority with the people.

Legitimate power required consent.

CHANGE

People could alter or abolish a government that violated their rights.

Its authority depended on consent.

A FIrm league

After independence, Americans needed cooperation among the states, but their experience with Britain made concentrated national power appear dangerous.

The ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION created a loose union. Every state retained STATE SOVEREIGNTY and received one vote in a UNICAMERAL CONGRESS, regardless of population. Congress possessed only the powers the states granted it.

Fear of tyranny produced a deliberately weak national government. Its limited powers mattered when Congress confronted problems affecting the entire country.

SECTION 2 · ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION

The Limits of Congress

CONGRESS COULD

  • Declare war
  • Conduct diplomacy
  • Coin money
  • Request state money and troops

CONGRESS COULD NOT

  • Collect taxes directly
  • Regulate interstate trade
  • Use an executive to enforce decisions
  • Use national courts to settle disputes
  • Control coinage exclusively

[ADD SQUARE IMAGE]

The Common defense

Congress could declare war, conduct diplomacy, coin money, and request resources. However, it could not collect taxes directly, regulate INTERSTATE COMMERCE, or compel states to follow national decisions.

Without an executive, national policies lacked dependable enforcement. Without national courts, disputes lacked consistent interpretation. Congress also depended on states for troops and funding.

SHAYS’ REBELLION exposed these weaknesses. National responsibilities required usable national powers, and the Articles did not provide enough of them.

When Congress Could Not Act

ARTICLES WEAKNESS

NATIONAL CONSEQUENCE

No direct taxation

No commerce power

Competing state rules disrupted interstate commerce.

No executive or judiciary

National decisions lacked enforcement and consistent interpretation.

Unanimous amendments

One state could block reform.

No centralized military power

A more perfect union

In 1787, delegates gathered to revise the Articles. They instead created a new CONSTITUTION that allowed the national government to tax, regulate INTERSTATE COMMERCE, enforce laws, and operate federal courts.

The delegates also limited the stronger government. SEPARATION OF POWERS divided responsibilities among three branches, while CHECKS AND BALANCES allowed each branch to restrain the others.

FEDERALISM divided authority between national and state governments. Power increased, but it did not remain in one place.

SECTION 4 · CONSTITUTIONAL CONVENTION

Power Was Divided Twice

WITHIN THE NATIONAL GOVERNMENT

SEPARATION OF POWERS gave different responsibilities to Congress, the president, and federal courts.

CHECKS AND BALANCES allowed the branches to restrain one another. No branch controlled the entire process.

BETWEEN LEVELS OF GOVERNMENT

FEDERALISM divided authority between the national and state governments.

The national government gained new powers. States retained authority over their own responsibilities.

[ADD SQUARE IMAGE]

Mutual Deference

Delegates could not complete the Constitution without settling disputes over representation, slavery, and presidential selection. Their compromises shaped who received influence in the new system.

The GREAT COMPROMISE created a BICAMERAL LEGISLATURE with population-based House representation and equal state representation in the Senate. The ELECTORAL COLLEGE established an indirect, state-based presidential selection process.

The THREE-FIFTHS COMPROMISE and the agreement protecting slave importation until 1808 increased slaveholding states’ influence. Compromise made agreement possible while distributing power unequally.

SECTION 5 · COMPROMISES

Agreement Reshaped Political Power

WHAT EACH AGREEMENT CHANGED

GREAT COMPROMISE
House seats followed population. Every state received two senators. Large and small states gained influence in different chambers.

ELECTORAL COLLEGE
State electors selected the president. Each state’s total reflected its House and Senate representation.

THREE-FIFTHS COMPROMISE
Three-fifths of enslaved populations counted for representation and direct taxation. Slaveholding states gained power without granting enslaved people rights.

Extend the sphere

The proposed Constitution produced a debate over whether stronger national authority would protect liberty or become another source of tyranny.

In FEDERALIST NO. 10, Madison argued that a large republic would contain many competing FACTIONS, making majority domination harder. BRUTUS NO. 1 argued that a large republic would distance representatives from citizens.

Brutus also warned that the NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE and SUPREMACY CLAUSE could expand national authority. Both sides defended liberty through opposing theories of power.

SECTION 6 · RATIFICATION DEBATE

Two Arguments About Liberty

FEDERALIST NO. 10

POSITION
Supported ratification and stronger national authority.

CORE ARGUMENT
Many competing interests made it harder for a majority FACTION to threaten others’ rights.

More interests made domination harder.

BRUTUS NO. 1

POSITION
Opposed ratification and favored state authority.

CORE ARGUMENT
Distant representatives weakened accountability. The NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE and SUPREMACY CLAUSE could expand national power and threaten rights.

[ADD SQUARE IMAGE]

nine states

Article VII required nine states to ratify the Constitution. FEDERALISTS supported the stronger national government, while ANTI-FEDERALISTS feared threats to state authority and individual liberty.

The original Constitution contained several protections, but it lacked a separate list of individual rights. FEDERALISTS promised that the new government would add one after ratification.

The ninth state ratified in 1788. The government began operating in 1789, and the BILL OF RIGHTS followed in 1791. Ratification joined stronger government with promised protections.

LESSON RECAP

The United States replaced the weak national government by granting effective national powers while dividing authority and adding protections intended to guard against tyranny.

  • ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION → limited national power
  • Articles weaknesses → stronger powers under the CONSTITUTION
  • SEPARATION OF POWERS + CHECKS AND BALANCES + FEDERALISM → divided authority
  • Ratification debate → BILL OF RIGHTS protections for individual liberty
