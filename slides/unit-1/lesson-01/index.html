THE BRIDGE

You just told a king that some freedoms are not his to give. That claim points toward the first of six DEMOCRATIC IDEALS, beliefs about what government should be, do, and protect.

The six explain why government exists, who gives it power, what limits that power, and how people should be treated.

We Hold These Truths

In July 1776, thirteen colonies told one of the world’s most powerful kings: You no longer have the right to rule us.

But the Declaration of Independence was more than a breakup letter. It was a carefully built argument.

First, it explains what people are owed and why government exists. Then, it accuses King George III of betraying those purposes. Finally, colonial representatives announce that British rule is over.

At its center is a radical claim: Government does not own the people. It works for them.

Jefferson did not invent that idea. To find its roots, we need to travel back before 1776.

By Nature

In the late 1600s, English philosopher John Locke challenged DIVINE RIGHT, the belief that kings received authority from God.

Locke pictured people living before government. They were “by nature, all free, equal, and independent.” Each INDIVIDUAL already possessed LIFE, the right to live and be safe; LIBERTY, the freedom to make your own choices; and PROPERTY, including your belongings, land, and what you produced through your work.

Locke called these NATURAL RIGHTS because people possessed them from birth, not because a ruler handed them out.

Created equal

Jefferson used Locke’s ideas to explain why the colonies should break away from Britain. The Declaration says that “all men are created equal” and are “endowed” with UNALIENABLE RIGHTS, including life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

Here, EQUALITY meant that no one was born with the right to rule someone else. It did not mean everyone had the same money, abilities, or influence.

But the new nation did not treat everyone this way. Slavery continued. Women could not vote. Indigenous people were pushed outside the political community. The Declaration announced an ideal that the country had not yet made real.

COLLABORATIVE BRAINSTORM

these ends

The Declaration says people have rights before any government is created. But people also need safety. Thomas Hobbes warned that life without government could become “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.”

People solve this problem by making a SOCIAL CONTRACT. They agree to follow common laws and give government the power to enforce them. In return, government protects their lives, liberty, and property.

Like any contract, both sides have responsibilities: people follow the laws, and government protects their rights.

Their Just Powers

The Declaration says government exists to protect people’s rights. Its power comes from the CONSENT OF THE GOVERNED, meaning people authorize the government to rule.

This is POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY. The people are the highest political authority, not a king.

But popular power does not make government unlimited. LIMITED GOVERNMENT means officials must obey the law and use power only for legitimate purposes.

The Declaration therefore treats British rule as a broken contract that's lost the CONSENT of the people.

Life, Liberty, Happiness

The Declaration connects the ideals we have studied:

People possess NATURAL RIGHTS.
Government exists through a SOCIAL CONTRACT.
Its power comes from POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY and the CONSENT OF THE GOVERNED.
Its authority must remain LIMITED.

In 1776, these were fighting words. The Declaration challenged the ancient belief that kings were born to rule and everyone else was born to obey.

It claimed that people could reject a government and build a new one.

WE The people

The U.S. CONSTITUTION was written in 1787 to create a stronger government after independence. Its opening section, the PREAMBLE, explains who creates government and what it should do.

It begins, “We the People.” The people create government to form a stronger Union, establish justice, keep peace, defend the country, promote the common good, and secure liberty.

These shared purposes make the Constitution a SOCIAL CONTRACT. Government exists to serve the people, not a king. The Preamble states the promises. The rest of the Constitution builds the system to carry them out.

EQ: How do landmark texts express America’s democratic ideals?

Secure the Blessings

The Constitution turns promises into a system.

The SOCIAL CONTRACT gives government authority to serve shared purposes. POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY places that authority with the people. LIMITED GOVERNMENT keeps officials within constitutional boundaries.

The Constitution also develops REPUBLICANISM. Citizens choose representatives to govern in their name. Those representatives make decisions and remain accountable through elections.

Republicanism gives people a practical way to take part in government.

A More Perfect Union

Enlightenment thinker MONTESQUIEU argued that power should restrain power.

The Constitution follows this idea through SEPARATION OF POWERS, dividing authority among three branches. CHECKS AND BALANCES lets each branch push back against the others.

Together, these principles protect LIMITED GOVERNMENT. Government can act, but no branch can rule alone.

Taken together, the Preamble and the Constitution’s structure reflect the DEMOCRATIC IDEALS. They turn those ideas into a government people can use and hold accountable.

Four Score and Seven

The DECLARATION OF INDEPENDENCE announced America’s DEMOCRATIC IDEALS in 1776. It claimed that people possessed rights and that government needed their consent. The U.S. CONSTITUTION created a system meant to protect those ideals in 1787.

By 1863, those promises faced their greatest crisis. The Civil War had already killed or wounded hundreds of thousands of Americans. The conflict began after years of disagreement over slavery, which denied millions the freedom and equality the nation claimed to value.

At Gettysburg, Lincoln spoke while the country was still fighting for its future.

EQ: How do landmark texts express America’s democratic ideals?

 

Conceived in liberty

Lincoln began by looking back to the nation’s founding. He said America was “conceived in Liberty” and dedicated to the belief that “all men are created equal.”

He was not simply repeating the Declaration. He was using its words to judge the country’s present crisis. If EQUALITY was a true democratic ideal, slavery could not continue while the nation called itself free.

Lincoln also described government as “of the people, by the people, for the people.” This expressed POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY. The people were supposed to hold political power and decide whether the nation’s founding ideals would survive.

A New Birth

Lincoln called the Civil War a “new birth of freedom.” A birth suggests that something new can emerge from a painful struggle.

He hoped the war would give the United States a second beginning. The nation would not merely restore the old Union, because that Union had allowed slavery to exist beside claims of liberty. Instead, the country could become more faithful to its founding ideals.

The phrase mattered because Lincoln gave the soldiers’ deaths a larger purpose. Their sacrifice could help create a democracy where EQUALITY and POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY were not just promises, but principles the nation worked to honor.

SYNTHESIS

In the Gettysburg Address, Abraham Lincoln connected EQUALITY and POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY to the fight against slavery. He argued that democracy could not survive while people were denied freedom and a voice in government.

 

Do you think the United States today lives up to those ideals? If not, are we the democracy the nation set out to become in 1776? Explain your thinking.

LESSON RECAP

Across three texts, American DEMOCRATIC IDEALS explain what government should protect, who gives it power, and how that power must be limited.

  • DECLARATION: States the founding logic of NATURAL RIGHTS, EQUALITY, SOCIAL CONTRACT, POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY, and LIMITED GOVERNMENT.
  • CONSTITUTION: Builds a government based on those ideals and adds REPUBLICANISM, allowing people to govern through representatives.
  • GETTYSBURG ADDRESS: Renews the promise of EQUALITY and POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY during the Civil War.

WORD WALL

NATURAL RIGHTS

Rights every person possesses by birth that government cannot rightfully take away.

SOCIAL CONTRACT

An agreement in which people accept government authority in exchange for order and protection of their rights.

POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY

The principle that government power comes from the consent of the people.

LIMITED GOVERNMENT

The principle that government power is restricted by law and cannot be absolute.

REPUBLICANISM

A system in which citizens choose representatives to make government decisions for them.

EQUALITY

The principle that all people possess equal human and political standing. No one is naturally born with the right to rule another.
